Deep Dive — Equity Risk Premium

No WoW movement registered across any pillar this week. In the absence of a differential mover, we focus on the pillar carrying the highest absolute score and the greatest forward-looking consequence: the Equity Risk Premium, which remains pinned at 10.0/10 — the ceiling of the scoring function.

The raw ERP reading of –0.74% means that the implied forward earnings yield on US large-cap equities now sits below the risk-free rate by 74 basis points. In plain terms, an investor buying the broad equity market at current valuations is accepting a negative spread over Treasuries — paying for the privilege of bearing equity risk. This is not unprecedented in short bursts, but its persistence at this magnitude is rare in the post-war record and was last observed in a sustained fashion in the late 1990s.

The sentinel tied to this pillar has been in alert since the ERP crossed below the 0.80% threshold. It remains the only active sentinel of the three monitored. What it tells us is structural: the cushion available to absorb an earnings miss, a rates shock, or a multiple compression event is effectively zero. Any repricing begins from a starting point of negative compensation for risk.

Critically, this pillar improves mechanically in a drawdown — as equities fall, the implied yield rises and the premium widens. This is by design: Gauge A measures fragility before the fact, not damage during it. A 10.0 score here is a statement about how far the market would have to fall to restore normal risk compensation, not a prediction that it will.

Editorially, the 41.5% equity weight is difficult to defend on a pure risk-compensation basis when the ERP is negative. A risk-first framework would argue for a meaningfully lower equity allocation — perhaps 30–33% — with the freed capital distributed to cash and short-duration sovereigns. The 14.5% cash position is the right instinct but undersized given the signal environment. However, the allocation is a product of the rules engine, not of this commentary, and the numbers above are what the portfolio holds. The system's posture is deliberate: it does not reduce equity exposure based on Gauge A alone, reserving defensive repositioning for a Gauge B firing — a design choice that accepts the possibility of participating in late-cycle upside at the cost of absorbing the initial leg of any drawdown.

CIO Verdict

Stasis at altitude. The system is flat week-over-week across every input — five pillars, three sentinels, two Gauge B triggers. This is a holiday-shortened data vacuum, not a signal of stability. The underlying condition has not changed: a negative equity risk premium, an extreme liquidity ratio, and a yield curve that offers minimal term compensation. These are the preconditions for a disorderly repricing, not evidence that one is imminent.

The portfolio remains fully invested at its macro allocation. The CIO's view is that the equity weight is too high for the fragility environment described by Gauge A, but the system does not act on that assessment until Gauge B confirms concurrent stress. That discipline — refusing to front-run a recession that may not arrive — is the architecture's primary strength. It also means that if and when a drawdown begins, the portfolio absorbs the first impact before rotating to its defensive vector. This is an accepted trade-off, not an oversight.

No action is required or warranted this week. The next scheduled semi-annual rebalance is 29 January 2027. Between now and then, the only event that changes the portfolio is a Gauge B firing.
