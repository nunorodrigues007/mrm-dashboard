Executive Summary

The Global Resilience Score holds at 7.0 for a second consecutive week, with all five pillars unchanged. The composite remains deep in the Turbulence band — a reflection of how much latent fragility has accumulated over the medium-term horizon, not a statement about whether a downturn is underway. No pillar moved this week. The flat read across all five inputs is itself unusual: it typically reflects a market coasting on momentum with no new macro data forcing a reassessment. That condition can persist for weeks or end abruptly. The system does not forecast which.

Deep Dive — Equity Risk Premium

Why the ERP earns the spotlight this week: With no pillar registering a week-over-week change, the deep dive defaults to the pillar carrying the highest absolute score. The Equity Risk Premium sits at a perfect 10.0/10 — the maximum fragility reading the model assigns — on a raw value of –1.17%. A negative ERP means equity investors are, in aggregate, accepting a lower expected return than the risk-free rate. By any historical standard this is an extreme condition.

What it measures: The ERP is constructed as the difference between the forward earnings yield on the broad equity market and the yield on intermediate-duration government bonds. At –1.17%, equities offer roughly 117 basis points less than Treasuries on a static-earnings basis. The last time the premium was this compressed was the late-1990s technology bubble, and the current reading has persisted for multiple weeks without correction.

What it does not measure: It does not measure whether equities will decline next week or next quarter. A negative ERP can coexist with rising equity prices for extended periods — it did so in 1998-99 and it is doing so now. The pillar captures the magnitude of the repricing that would be required to restore the premium to its long-run median, and the answer is: substantial.

Implications for the framework: The ERP pillar is the single largest contributor to the composite's 7.0 reading. If it were to normalise — through lower equity prices, higher earnings, or lower bond yields — the composite would fall materially. Conversely, further compression from here has negligible effect on the score because it is already at ceiling. The asymmetry is the point: risk is priced for perfection, and perfection has a short half-life.

CIO Verdict

A high Gauge A score with Gauge B off is the system working as designed, not a contradiction. The composite at 7.0 says the structural cushion is thin — valuations are stretched, the risk premium is negative, and leverage is elevated. But stretched is not broken. The concurrent triggers exist precisely to distinguish between a market that is expensive and one that is actively deteriorating. Today it is the former.

The portfolio holds. No rebalance was triggered and none is pending until the next semi-annual date in January 2027, unless Gauge B fires first. The –8.09% alpha deficit versus the broad equity index is the cost of carrying 14.5% cash and 19.7% Treasuries into a market that has continued to reward undifferentiated risk-taking. That cost is tolerable for a framework whose value proposition is measured in drawdown avoidance across full cycles, not in relative return during late-stage expansions.

Watch list for the weeks ahead: initial claims for any uptick toward 225K+, bank delinquency data when Q2 2026 figures are released, and any earnings-driven compression of the already-negative ERP. None of these are forecasts. They are the inputs that would move the numbers you see above.
